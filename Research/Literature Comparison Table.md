## Streaming lessons for DICOM

Video and light-field streaming techniques are relevant, but they cannot be transferred unchanged. The useful transferable ideas are:

- **Buffer management:** maintain a bounded set of prefetched frames or tiles and evict lower-value data.
- **Prediction uncertainty:** use confidence or probability rather than assuming the next requested image is known exactly.
- **Priority regions:** distinguish the current viewport, neighboring/predicted content, and low-priority content.
- **Adaptive prefetching:** vary prefetch depth according to bandwidth, latency, buffer occupancy, and prediction confidence.
- **Refresh versus new retrieval:** decide whether to replace buffered data with more accurate or more relevant data.
- **Stall avoidance:** preserve enough data for immediate interaction before speculative prefetching.
- **Cache-aware decisions:** account for what is already cached instead of treating every request as an origin request.
- **Low-latency switching:** use small retrieval units and suitable boundaries to reduce viewport-change delay.
- **Layered client caching:** separate compressed/persistent data from decoded/in-memory data, as the light-field work separates disk and GPU caches.
- **Segment locality:** group data likely to be used together, but validate grouping against DICOM frame/tile semantics rather than copying video segmentation directly.

For DICOM, the likely units are:

```text
DICOM frame
DICOM tile
viewport region
resolution level
rendered image
compressed representation
```

DICOM is not ordinary video. A diagnostic viewer must preserve pixel fidelity, frame identity, spatial metadata, orientation, and clinically meaningful transformations. Video techniques should therefore guide **prefetching and buffer management**, not justify silently substituting image content or lowering diagnostic quality.

## Recommended role in the dissertation

Use the streaming literature to design the **retrieval and prefetching policy**, not to replace the cybersecurity research question.

A suitable adaptation is:

```text
Current viewport
    → highest priority
Adjacent/predicted frames or tiles
    → prefetch if confidence and resources justify it
Distant or low-probability data
    → defer or retrieve at lower priority
Persistent cache
    → encrypt, limit, expire, and measure for recoverability
Decoded working set
    → keep bounded in RAM/GPU memory
```

The dissertation experiment can compare:

- No prefetching versus rule-based prefetching.
- Fixed prefetch depth versus confidence-aware prefetching.
- RAM-only versus persistent cache.
- Unencrypted versus encrypted persistent cache.
- Cold, warm, reload, logout, and browser-restart conditions.
- Single-layer complete-image retrieval versus frame/tile or segmented retrieval, where the backend supports it.

The cybersecurity contribution remains the measurement of data exposure and protection. Streaming research supplies the performance and prefetching techniques that make the comparison realistic.

## Working research gap hypothesis

The literature demonstrates zero-footprint medical-image viewers, DICOMweb interoperability, client-side rendering, caching, prefetching, progressive image streaming, tile-based video adaptation, light-field segment streaming, encrypted web storage, and secure clinical workflows. A working hypothesis is that integrated cybersecurity evidence is still limited for how video-inspired buffer, segment, and prefetching strategies interact with RAM, browser cache, and OPFS when storing DICOM frames or tiles.

> Working hypothesis: adapting selected streaming concepts—bounded buffers, prediction confidence, priority-based prefetching, cache-aware retrieval, layered client caches, and refresh-versus-new-fetch decisions—may improve performance while still requiring explicit measurement of confidentiality and persistence risks in DICOM browser caches.
